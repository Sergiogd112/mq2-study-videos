2. Euler's turbomachinery equation

1 · Fundamentals
  • Torque = change of angular momentum: τA = ṁ(re ve − ri vi); power Ẇ = ω τA
  • First law: Ẇc = ṁ(hte − hti) ⇒ hte − hti = ω(re ve − ri vi)
  • Mean line (rm = (rt + rh)/2, U = ω rm): compressor cp ΔTt = U(ve − vi); turbine cp ΔTt = U(vi − ve)
  • Work depends ONLY on blade speed and the change of TANGENTIAL (swirl) velocity
  • High U = high work per stage, limited by blade stress (AN²)

Equations

τA​=m˙(re​ve​−ri​vi​),W˙into fluid​=ωτA​⇒hte​−hti​=ω(re​ve​−ri​vi​)
power delivered TO the fluid (compressor sense; with the Ẇ > 0 extracted convention of part 1 this is −Ẇ)
compressor: cp​ΔTt​=U(v2​−v1​),turbine: cp​ΔTt​=U(v2​+v3​)
mean line, U = ω r_m
Narration

Everything starts with Euler's equation. The torque on the fluid equals the change of its angular momentum, m dot times r v at exit minus r v at inlet, where v is the tangential velocity, and the power is omega times the torque. Equating with the first law, the change of total enthalpy equals omega times the change of r v. At the mean radius, with the blade speed U equal to omega times r m, the compressor work per unit mass is U times the increase of swirl, and the turbine work is U times the decrease of swirl. So the work depends only on the blade speed and the tangential velocities, not on the axial velocity or the blade shape, and high blade speed is the most direct route to high stage work, until blade stress stops you.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §1.2

3. Conventions that prevent sign errors

1 · Fundamentals
  • Compressor stage: ROTOR then stator (1→2 rotor, 2→3 stator); turbine stage: STATOR then rotor (1→2 nozzle, 2→3 rotor)
  • V = VR + U; axial u common to both frames; angles from the AXIAL direction: v = u tan α, vR = u tan β
  • P, T, ρ frame-invariant; Tt, Pt, M are NOT (Tt1R ≠ Tt1)
  • Compressor: v + vR = U at every station
  • Turbine: v2 along U, v3 positive AGAINST U: v2R = v2 − U, v3R = v3 + U

Equations

V=VR​+U,v=utanα,vR​=utanβ,compressor: v+vR​=U
Narration

Most mistakes in turbomachinery are about conventions, so fix them now. A compressor stage is a rotor followed by a stator, while a turbine stage is a stator, or nozzle, followed by a rotor, so station 2 is the rotor exit in a compressor but the rotor inlet in a turbine, and these local stations have nothing to do with the cycle stations. Absolute and relative velocities are related by V equals V R plus U, the axial component u is the same in both frames, and all angles are measured from the axial direction, alpha absolute and beta relative. Static properties are the same in both frames, but total temperature, total pressure and Mach number are not, which is why the rotor must always be analysed in its own frame. In a compressor the tangential components satisfy v plus v R equals U; in a turbine, following Mattingly, v 2 is measured along U and v 3 against it, so v 2 R equals v 2 minus U and v 3 R equals v 3 plus U, and the two swirls add in the work.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §2.1–2.3

4. Compressor stage: work and thermodynamics

2 · Compressor stage
  • cp ΔTt = U(v2 − v1) = U u (tan β1 − tan β2) = U u (tan α2 − tan α1); Tt3 = Tt2
  • Rotor (relative frame): Tt2R = Tt1R = T1 + V1R²/(2cp); Pt2R = Pt1R · LR
  • Stator: Tt3 = Tt2; Pt3 = Pt2 · LS
  • Loss coefficients: ϕcr = (Pt1R − Pt2R)/(½ρ1V1R²), ϕcs = (Pt2 − Pt3)/(½ρ2V2²); LR = 1 − ϕcr γP1M1R²/(2Pt1R)
  • Both rows DIFFUSE the flow (V2R < V1R, V3 < V2): this limits the pressure rise per stage

Equations

cp​ΔTt​=U(v2​−v1​)=Uu(tanβ1​−tanβ2​)=Uu(tanα2​−tanα1​)
Tt1R​=T1​+2cp​V1R2​​=Tt2R​,Pt2R​=LR​Pt1R​,LR​=1−ϕcr​2Pt1R​γP1​M1R2​​
Narration

In a compressor stage, only the rotor does work: c p delta T t equals U times v 2 minus v 1, which can be written with absolute angles, as adding swirl, or with relative angles, as removing relative swirl, which is paid for with diffusion. Across the rotor, in its own frame, nothing does work, so the relative total temperature is conserved, while the relative total pressure drops by the rotor loss ratio L R. Across the stator, total temperature is conserved and total pressure drops by L S. The losses are quantified by loss coefficients: the total pressure drop divided by the inlet dynamic pressure in the row's frame, which converts into L R equals one minus phi times gamma P 1 M 1 R squared over two P t 1 R, the form used in exercise 2 A. Both rows decelerate the flow in their own frame, against an adverse pressure gradient, and that is why a compressor needs many stages while one or two turbine stages can drive it.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §3.3–3.4

5. Stage parameters and limits

2 · Compressor stage
  • πcs, τcs, ηcs = (πcs^((γ−1)/γ) − 1)/(τcs − 1), ecs = ((γ−1)/γ) ln πcs / ln τcs
  • Degree of reaction Rc = (T2 − T1)/(T3 − T1): 0.5 shares the diffusion (Farokhi: ≈ 0.6)
  • Loading Ψ = cp ΔTt / U² (HPC 0.30–0.35); flow coefficient Φ = u/U (HPC 0.45–0.55)
  • Diffusion factor D = 1 − Ve/Vi + |vi − ve|/(2σVi) ≤ 0.6 (Lieblein); de Haller Ve/Vi ≥ 0.72 is only a rough check
  • Blade stress AN² = 3600·10³ (σc/ρ) / [π(1 + At/Ah)], σc/ρ in MPa/(g/cm³)

Equations

D=1−Vi​Ve​​+2σVi​∣vi​−ve​∣​

Narration

A stage is characterised by a few dimensionless groups. The stage pressure and temperature ratios give its isentropic and polytropic efficiencies with the same formulas as for a whole compressor. The degree of reaction is the fraction of the static temperature rise that happens in the rotor; 0.5 shares the diffusion equally, and Farokhi argues for about 0.6 because rotating boundary layers tolerate adverse gradients slightly better. The stage loading coefficient Psi is c p delta T t over U squared, typically 0.30 to 0.35 in a high pressure compressor, and the flow coefficient Phi is u over U, typically 0.45 to 0.55. The diffusion factor, due to Lieblein, measures the deceleration on the suction side and must stay below about 0.6, while the de Haller ratio of 0.72 is only a rough sanity check. And the blade stress parameter, A N squared, links the annulus area and the shaft speed to the specific strength the material must provide.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §3.6

6. Cascade geometry: solidity, incidence, deviation

2 · Compressor stage
  • Solidity σ = c/s, pitch s = 2πr_m/n_b; axial solidity σx = cx/s = σ cos θ (stagger θ)
  • Flow angles (α, or β in the rotor) differ from the blade METAL angles γ
  • Incidence i = α_i − γ_i ≈ 0 at design
  • Deviation δ = α_e − γ_e: compressor (Carter) δ = (γ_i − γ_e)/(4√σ); turbine δ = (γ_i + γ_e)/(8√σ), small
  • Higher σ: better guidance, less deviation, lower D, but more blades, weight and friction

Equations

σ=sc​,s=nb​2πrm​​,i=αi​−γi​,δ=αe​−γe​
δcompressor​=4σ​γi​−γe​​,δturbine​=8σ​γi​+γe​​
Carter's rule
Narration

The mean-line analysis gives flow angles, but the blades are drawn with metal angles, and the cascade vocabulary connects the two. The solidity sigma is the chord over the pitch, where the pitch is two pi r m over the number of blades; referred to the axial chord it is the axial solidity, sigma times the cosine of the stagger angle. The flow does not follow the blade exactly, so we distinguish the flow angles, alpha in the stator or beta in the rotor, from the metal angles gamma, drawn dotted in the sketch. At the leading edge the difference is the incidence, alpha i minus gamma i, which is nearly zero at design. At the trailing edge the flow under-turns by the deviation, alpha e minus gamma e: Carter's rule gives the camber over four root sigma for a compressor, while in a turbine the accelerating flow keeps it small, the sum of the metal angles over eight root sigma. Packing the blades more closely reduces deviation and the diffusion factor, at the price of more blades, weight and friction; the Zweifel criterion of the turbine fixes exactly this solidity.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §3.5 eq. 3.26–3.27, §4.11 eq. 4.21–4.22, eq. 4.56

7. Repeating-row, repeating-stage design

2 · Compressor stage
  • α1 = β2 = α3, β1 = α2 = β3, u constant: mirror triangles, stage can be stacked
  • v1 + v2 = U = ωr; cp ΔTt = V2² − V1²: same ΔTt for EVERY stage
  • τcs = 1 + (γ−1)M1²/[1 + (γ−1)/2 M1²] · (cos²α1/cos²α2 − 1)
  • ωr/V1 = cos α1 (tan α1 + tan α2); Ψ = (tan α2 − tan α1)/(tan α1 + tan α2), Φ = 1/(tan α1 + tan α2)
  • Rc = 0.5 exactly; ec = ecs (polytropic carries from stage to machine)

Equations

v1​+v2​=U,cp​ΔTt​=V22​−V12​
τcs​=1+1+2γ−1​M12​(γ−1)M12​​(cos2α2​cos2α1​​−1),V1​ωr​=cosα1​(tanα1​+tanα2​)
Ψ=tanα1​+tanα2​tanα2​−tanα1​​,Φ=tanα1​+tanα2​1​
Narration

A powerful simplification is the repeating-row, repeating-stage design: the stage exit velocity and angle equal those at its inlet, using mirror-image rows, so stages can be stacked indefinitely. Then v 1 plus v 2 equals the blade speed, and the work becomes V 2 squared minus V 1 squared over c p: the same temperature rise for every stage, a consequence of the geometry, not an extra assumption. The stage temperature ratio depends only on the inlet Mach number and the two flow angles. The ratio of wheel speed to inlet velocity, and the loading and flow coefficients, become purely geometric functions of alpha 1 and alpha 2. The degree of reaction is exactly one half, and the polytropic efficiency of a stage equals that of the whole compressor, which is why it is the efficiency carried from stage to machine.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §3.7

8. Reading the design chart

2 · Compressor stage
  • Chart = repeating stage for given D, σ, ec: α2 (right axis), ωr/V1 (green), πcs per M1 (blues)
  • Mechanical route: U known (shaft, radius) → pick α1 → read ωr/V1 → V1, M1 → πcs, α2
  • Aerodynamic route: target πcs → any intersection with a Mach line → α1, α2, M1 → U
  • Higher M1 → higher πcs; high α1 needs a large wheel speed
  • Keep M1 ≈ 0.4–0.6 and α1 modest (guide vanes)
Narration

Because the repeating stage depends on only a few numbers, it is tabulated as a design chart, like this one from exercise 2 A. Against the inlet angle alpha 1, it shows the exit angle alpha 2 on the right axis, the wheel speed ratio omega r over V 1 in green, and the stage pressure ratio for several inlet Mach numbers in blue. There are two ways to use it: if the blade speed is fixed by the shaft and the radius, choose alpha 1, read omega r over V 1, get V 1 and the Mach number, and then the stage pressure ratio and alpha 2. If instead a pressure ratio is the target, every intersection with a Mach number line is a valid design, which fixes the angles and the Mach number, and the chart then tells you the blade speed needed. The trends: a higher inlet Mach number gives more pressure ratio per stage, and a large alpha 1 requires a high wheel speed; keep M 1 between 0.4 and 0.6.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §3.8; Exercise 2A figure 1

9. Multistage compressors

2 · Compressor stage
  • Same ΔTt per stage: Tt,exit = Tt1 + ns ΔTt
  • πc = [(Tt1 + ns ΔTt)/Tt1]^(γ ec/(γ−1))
  • Stage ratios FALL along the machine (Tt1 grows): πc ≠ πcs^ns
  • Number of stages: ns ≈ Tt1(πc^((γ−1)/(γ ec)) − 1) / ΔTt,stage, rounded up
  • Mattingly's πc = πcs^N is only a device for relating η to ηs

Equations

πc​=[Tt1​Tt1​+ns​ΔTt​​]γ−1γec​​,ns​≈ΔTt,stage​Tt1​(πc(γ−1)/(γec​)​−1)​
Narration

A multistage compressor stacks identical repeating stages, each adding the same total temperature rise. So the exit total temperature is the inlet one plus n s times delta T t, and the overall pressure ratio follows from the polytropic efficiency: the temperature ratio raised to gamma e c over gamma minus one. Because the inlet temperature of each stage grows, its temperature ratio and pressure ratio fall along the machine, so the overall ratio is not the first stage ratio to the power n s; in the manual's example the stage ratio falls from 1.25 to 1.15. Inverting the formula gives the number of stages for a target pressure ratio, rounded up to an integer. The manual warns that Mattingly also writes pi c equals pi c s to the N, but that is only a device for relating efficiencies and is inconsistent with real repeating triangles.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §3.9

10. Radial variation: free vortex and the hub

2 · Compressor stage
  • Same Δv at every radius → work ∝ r → endwall recirculation
  • Radial equilibrium: dh_t/dr = u du/dr + v dv/dr + v²/r; uniform work r(v₂ − v₁) = const ⇒ impose dh_t/dr = 0
  • Free vortex (r v = const): constant u; R = 1 − (1 − R_m)(r_m/r)²
  • Reaction is LOWEST at the hub: long compressor blades can go negative there
  • Turbine: shorter blades, milder; keep R_t > −0.15 at the hub
  • Sizing: A from the MFP, r_t = √(A / [π(1 − (r_h/r_t)²)]), h = A/(2πr_m); hub/tip 0.60–0.75 at HPC inlet

Equations

drdht​​=udrdu​+vdrdv​+rv2​
R=1−(1−Rm​)(rrm​​)2
free vortex r v = const, constant u
Narration

So far everything happened at the mean radius, but a blade spans from hub to tip. If the same swirl change were imposed at every radius, Euler's equation would give a work proportional to the radius, much larger at the tip, and the resulting pressure gradients would drive recirculation at both walls. So the design keeps the work uniform along the span, r times the swirl change constant, combined with the radial equilibrium equation for constant total enthalpy. The simplest solution is the free vortex, r v constant, which keeps the axial velocity uniform and gives a reaction of one minus one minus the mean reaction, times r m over r squared. As the plot shows, the reaction is lowest at the hub and highest at the tip, and on a long compressor blade it can become negative at the hub, which is why other swirl distributions are used when the hub to tip ratio is low. In a turbine the blades are shorter, so the variation is milder, and the usual criterion is to keep the hub reaction above about minus 0.15. Finally, the hub and tip radii follow from the annulus area given by the mass flow parameter and the chosen hub to tip ratio, typically 0.6 to 0.75 at a compressor inlet.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §3.11 eq. 3.53–3.57, §4.20 eq. 4.62, eq. 3.21

14. 2A: the stage T-s diagram (template)

3 · Exercise 2A
  • Start with the static states 1 → 2 → 3 on their isobars
  • Totals sit V²/(2cp) above their statics, at the same entropy
  • Rotor: relative total t1R → t2R stays at constant T (Tt,R conserved), Pt,R falls by LR
  • Absolute total rises t1 → t2: the work ΔTt = 16.4 K

Narration

The exercises ask for a T s sketch of the stage, so here is a template drawn to scale with the 2 A numbers. Start with the three static states on their isobars: the static temperature rises through the rotor and again through the stator, because both rows diffuse. Each total state sits above its static state by V squared over two c p, at the same entropy, because a total state is reached isentropically. In the rotor frame, the relative total temperature is the same at 1 R and 2 R, a horizontal line, while the relative total pressure drops a little by the loss ratio. In the absolute frame the rotor adds work: the total temperature rises from t 1 to t 2 by 16.4 kelvin. Through the stator the absolute total temperature is constant and the total pressure falls slightly, while the static temperature climbs back as the flow decelerates to V 1. And every step moves to the right: each loss is an entropy increase, which is how you show the losses on the sketch.

17. Turbines: a different kind of machine

4 · Turbine stage
  • Stator (nozzle) then rotor; the flow ACCELERATES in both rows: favourable gradient, no stall
  • Large turning and pressure drop per stage: one or two stages drive a ten-stage compressor
  • Limited by temperature and stress, not by separation: cooling air is a cycle penalty
  • Euler: cp ΔTt = U(v2 + v3) = U(u2 tan α2 + u3 tan α3): the two swirls ADD
  • Nozzle: Tt2 = Tt1, Pt2 = Pt1 LS; rotor: Tt3R = Tt2R, Pt3R = Pt2R LR

Equations

cp​ΔTt​=U(v2​+v3​)=U(u2​tanα2​+u3​tanα3​)
Narration

The turbine is not a compressor run backwards. Its stage is a nozzle followed by a rotor, and the flow accelerates through both, under a favourable pressure gradient, so the boundary layers stay thin and there is nothing like compressor stall. That allows large turning and large pressure drops per stage, which is why one or two turbine stages drive a ten-stage compressor. Instead, the turbine is limited by temperature and stress: the gas is hotter than any blade material can bear, so the blades are cooled with compressor air, which is a direct penalty on the cycle. Euler's equation for the stage is c p delta T t equals U times v 2 plus v 3: with our sign convention the inlet and exit swirls add, and that is why a turbine stage extracts so much more work than a compressor stage absorbs. The nozzle conserves total temperature and loses total pressure, and the rotor conserves relative total temperature and loses relative total pressure.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §4.1–4.6

18. Loading, flow coefficient and reaction

4 · Turbine stage
  • Ψ = cp ΔTt / U², Φ = u2/U; constant u: Ψ/Φ = tan β2 + tan β3, Rt/Φ = (tan β3 − tan β2)/2
  • Zero reaction (impulse): β3 = β2; with zero exit swirl Ψ = 2, v2 = 2U
  • 50 % reaction: symmetric (α2 = β3, α3 = β2); with zero exit swirl Ψ = 1
  • Zero exit swirl, any reaction: Rt = 1 − Ψ/2 (high loading ⇔ low reaction)
  • α2 ≤ 70° (else V2 and losses too large); Ψ ≈ 2 is the ceiling → more work needs more stages
  • Choking check: 1st-stage nozzle M2 > 1 AND rotor-exit M3R < 0.9 (tip); 50 %: V3R = V2, T3 < T2 ⇒ M3R > M2 → cannot meter

Equations

Ψ=U2cp​ΔTt​​,Φ=Uu2​​,ΦΨ​=tanβ2​+tanβ3​,ΦRt​​=2tanβ3​−tanβ2​​
Rt​=1−2Ψ​
zero exit swirl
Narration

Turbine stages are characterised by the loading coefficient, c p delta T t over U squared, and the flow coefficient, u over U, and with constant axial velocity both ratios Psi over Phi and R t over Phi depend only on the relative angles. In a zero-reaction, or impulse, stage the relative angles are equal and the rotor only turns the flow; with zero exit swirl it delivers Psi equal to 2. In a 50 % reaction stage the triangles are symmetric, like the repeating compressor stage, and with zero exit swirl Psi equals 1: half the work of the impulse stage at the same blade speed, but with much lower velocities and losses. In general, with zero exit swirl, R t equals one minus Psi over two: high loading means low reaction, which is the fundamental trade between weight, through fewer stages, and efficiency. Two hard limits: the nozzle exit angle alpha 2 should not exceed about 70 degrees, and Psi of about 2 is the ceiling, since beyond it the rotor would have to diffuse. Finally the manual’s choking check: the first stage nozzle must reach M 2 above one to meter the engine flow, while the relative Mach number at the rotor exit must stay subsonic, below about 0.9 at the tip; in a 50 % reaction stage V 3 R equals V 2 and T 3 is lower than T 2, so M 3 R is even larger than M 2 and the two conditions cannot hold together, which is why a 50 % stage cannot meter the flow; exercise 3 A does reach an M 2 of 1.11, but only with an M 3 R of 1.21, an artefact of its unrealistic blade speed.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §4.10, §4.12, §4.15

19. The turbine design map

4 · Turbine stage
  • Axes: total turning ε = β2 + β3 vs β2; contours Ψ/Φ (solid) and Rt/Φ (dashed)
  • Zero reaction → Rt/Φ = 0 line (ε = 2β2)
  • 50 % reaction, zero exit swirl → β2 = 0 edge (ε = β3 = α2)
  • Pick the LARGEST Ψ/Φ that satisfies α2 ≤ 70° and the Zweifel limit
  • Then Φ = 1/(tan α2 − tan β2), U = √(cp ΔTt / Ψ), u = Φ U

Equations

Φ=tanα2​−tanβ2​1​,U=Ψcp​ΔTt​​​,u=ΦU
Narration

With constant axial velocity, the whole design space collapses onto this map: the total turning epsilon, beta 2 plus beta 3, against beta 2, with contours of Psi over Phi and of R t over Phi. Choosing the stage type fixes a locus: a zero-reaction stage lives on the R t over Phi equal to zero line, where epsilon is twice beta 2. A 50 % reaction stage with zero exit swirl lives on the left edge, beta 2 equal to zero, where the turning equals alpha 2. Along that locus, each intersection with a Psi over Phi contour is a candidate, and the usual choice in the exercises is the largest Psi over Phi that respects alpha 2 below 70 degrees and the Zweifel limit; since the reaction already fixes Psi and the cycle fixes the work, this choice sets the flow coefficient and the shape of the triangles, with lower axial velocity and more turning. Then the flow coefficient follows from the angles, the blade speed from the temperature drop that the cycle demands, U equals the square root of c p delta T t over Psi, and the axial velocity is Phi times U.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §4.14, fig. 4.14

20. Zweifel coefficient, AN² and materials

4 · Turbine stage
  • Zr = (2s/cx) cos²β3 (tan β2 + tan β3) (constant u): actual / maximum tangential force
  • Design Z ≈ 0.8–1.0 → fixes the axial solidity cx/s: chart shows Zr·cx/s
  • σc ≈ ρω²A(1 + At/Ah)/(4π) → AN² = 3600·10³ (σc/ρ)/[π(1 + At/Ah)]
  • Annulus A = ṁ√(R Tt)/(Pt cos α MFP(M)); compare σc/ρ with alloys at the blade temperature
  • Too high? lower N, change taper, add a stage, revisit the cycle

Equations

Zr​=2cx​s​cos2β3​(tanβ2​+tanβ3​)
ρσc​​=3.6×106π(1+At​/Ah​)AN2​  [g/cm3MPa​],A=Pt​cosαMFP(M)m˙RTt​​​
Narration

Two more checks complete a turbine design. The Zweifel coefficient is the ratio of the actual tangential force on a blade to the maximum force a rectangular pressure distribution could give; for constant axial velocity it is two s over c x times cos squared beta 3 times tan beta 2 plus tan beta 3. Zweifel recommends values around 0.8 to 1, so for given angles it fixes the axial solidity; the chart of exercise 3 B plots Z r times c x over s against the two relative angles, with the allowed limit drawn on it. The second check is the centrifugal stress at the blade root, proportional to the density, the square of the speed and the annulus area, which rearranges into the A N squared parameter. The annulus area comes from the mass flow parameter at the rotor inlet, and the resulting specific strength must be compared with the alloys' curves at the blade temperature. If no material qualifies, lower the speed, change the blade taper, add a stage, or go back to the cycle.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: Turbomachinery (13 Oct 2026), §4.17–4.19; Exercise 3B figures

Narration

Before leaving the turbines, a few sanity checks that the exercises do not ask for but that show real understanding. In 3 A, a blade speed of 656 metres per second at 2 500 rpm means a mean radius of U over omega, 2.51 metres, and with an annulus of 0.23 square metres the blades would be only about 1.5 centimetres tall, which is not a real turbine. Its nozzle exit Mach number of 1.11 also comes with a rotor exit relative Mach number of 707 over 584, 1.21, above the 0.9 the manual allows. The strict 3 B design is worse: M 2 of 1.79 would need convergent-divergent vanes, and the rotor exit relative Mach number is 1.42. The relaxed 3 B design, with M 2 of 1.25 and a rotor exit relative Mach number of 0.74, is aerodynamically better, but it needs a specific strength of 81 instead of 60, so a designer would keep it only with a lower speed or more cooling. The manual's section on the optimal nozzle exit swirl Mach number ties these together: with zero exit swirl, M 2 sin alpha 2 equals 2 M T times one minus R t, where M T is the blade speed over the speed of sound at the nozzle exit, so the reaction and the blade Mach number set the swirl, and the nozzle exit Mach number is chosen for maximum work per unit mass flow. In the exam, solve exactly what the statement asks, and then add one line of sanity check like these: it costs a minute and shows the examiner you understand the result.
