2. Bypass ratio and the 1/(1+α) trap

1 · Turbofan
  • α = ṁsec / ṁcore; ṁcore = ṁ0/(1+α), ṁsec = α ṁ0/(1+α)
  • Fuel referred to the core: ṁf = f ṁcore = f ṁ0/(1+α)
  • TSFC = f / [(1+α) F/ṁ0] ← the most frequent error
  • Inputs: α, πc, πf, Tt4, P0/P9, P0/P19; outputs: F/ṁ0, TSFC, FR, efficiencies
  • α and πf compete for the same turbine power: they cannot both be pushed
α = 6CFM56 in 1A/1B24.29 kg/score flow of 170 kg/s145.71 kg/sbypass flow

Equations

α=m˙core​m˙sec​​,m˙core​=1+αm˙0​​,m˙f​=fm˙core​,TSFC=(1+α)F/m˙0​f​
Narration

A separate-exhaust turbofan splits the inlet flow into a core stream and a bypass, or secondary, stream, and the bypass ratio alpha is their ratio. So the core flow is m dot zero over one plus alpha, and the bypass flow is alpha times m dot zero over one plus alpha. Fuel is referred to the core, so the fuel flow is f times the core flow, and the T S F C carries a factor one plus alpha in the denominator: forgetting it is, according to the manual, the most frequent error in turbofan calculations. The turbofan has three design variables where the turbojet had two: besides the compressor pressure ratio and T t four, the fan pressure ratio and the bypass ratio, which are coupled through the turbine. In exercise 1 A, with alpha equal to 6 and a total flow of 170 kilograms per second, the core receives 24.29 and the bypass 145.71 kilograms per second.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: On-Design Aircraft Engine Analysis (14 Oct 2026), §6.1; Exercise 1A statement and official solution

3. Two spools: who drives whom

1 · Turbofan
  • HP spool: HPT drives HPC: ẆHPC = ηm,HP ẆHPT, ẆHPT = (1+f) ṁcore cpt (Tt4 − Tt45)
  • LP spool: LPT drives fan + LPC: Ẇfan + ẆLPC = ηm,LP ẆLPT, ẆLPT = (1+f) ṁcore cpt (Tt45 − Tt5)
  • Fan works on ALL the flow: Ẇfan = ṁ0 cpc (Tt13 − Tt2); LPC only on the core: ẆLPC = ṁcore cpc (Tt25 − Tt13)
  • OPR = πf πLPC πHPC (1A: πHPC = 30.6 / (1.32·5) = 4.636)
  • Each compressor starts from the temperature left by the previous one

Equations

W˙HPC​=ηm,HP​(1+f)m˙core​cpt​(Tt4​−Tt45​)
W˙fan​+W˙LPC​=ηm,LP​(1+f)m˙core​cpt​(Tt45​−Tt5​),W˙fan​=m˙0​cpc​(Tt13​−Tt2​),W˙LPC​=m˙core​cpc​(Tt25​−Tt13​)

Narration

Real turbofans have two spools turning at different speeds, and the exercises are posed that way. The high pressure spool is simple: the high pressure turbine drives only the high pressure compressor, so the compressor power equals eta m H times the turbine power, which gives T t four point five. The low pressure turbine drives both the fan and the low pressure compressor, working on gas already cooled by the high pressure turbine, which gives T t five. Careful with the mass flows: the fan compresses the whole inlet flow, while the low pressure compressor only compresses the core; mixing the two conventions changes the fan power by a factor one plus alpha. The overall pressure ratio is the product of the three compression ratios, so in exercise 1 A the high pressure compressor must provide 30.6 divided by 1.32 times 5, which is 4.636, and each compressor starts from the temperature the previous one left behind.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: On-Design Aircraft Engine Analysis (14 Oct 2026), §6.1.2

4. Two-stream thrust and the thrust ratio

1 · Turbofan
  • F = Fcore + Fsec; Fcore = ṁ9 v9 − ṁcore v0 + (P9 − P0)A9; Fsec = ṁ19 v19 − ṁsec v0 + (P19 − P0)A19
  • FR = (Fcore/ṁcore) / (Fsec/ṁsec): ratio of SPECIFIC thrusts (not Fcore/Fsec)
  • ΔEk = ½(ṁ9 v9² + ṁ19 v19² − ṁ0 v0²); ηT = ΔEk/(ṁf hPR), ηP = F v0/ΔEk
  • Unmatched nozzles: compute ηP with the full thrust (closed form assumes P9 = P19 = P0)
FR ≈ 1minimum TSFC (civil)FR = 10.4exercise 1A: core works hard+8 pointsηP error if pressure terms dropped

Equations

F=m˙9​v9​+m˙19​v19​−m˙0​v0​+(P9​−P0​)A9​+(P19​−P0​)A19​
FR=Fsec​/m˙sec​Fcore​/m˙core​​
thrust ratio = ratio of SPECIFIC thrusts
Narration

Thrust now comes from two streams, each with its own momentum, ram drag and pressure term. The thrust ratio F R compares how hard each stream works per unit of its own mass flow: it is the ratio of specific thrusts, not of thrusts, and values around one minimise fuel consumption in civil engines. The kinetic energy increment adds both streams, and the thermal and propulsive efficiencies follow as before. In the manual's worked example, using the closed-form propulsive efficiency with under-expanded nozzles loses eight percentage points, so always compute it from the full thrust. In exercise 1 A, F R comes out at about 10: the core stream works ten times harder per kilogram than the fan stream, which says there is room for a larger bypass ratio, exactly the trend of modern engines.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: On-Design Aircraft Engine Analysis (14 Oct 2026), §6.1.4–6.1.6; Exercise 1A (recomputed FR)

12. Mixed exhaust and turboshaft (in brief)

3 · Other engines
  • Mixed exhaust: core and bypass join in a mixer → one nozzle (military, afterburner)
  • Pressure matching Pt6 ≈ Pt16 fixes πt; the power balance then gives α: α is an OUTPUT
  • Mixing gains thrust (velocity mismatch removed): biggest at high Th/Tc, around α ≈ 2; civil engines skip it for duct weight and drag
  • Turboshaft: HPT drives the compressor, a free LPT drives the shaft; τtL is a design choice
  • PSFC = ṁf / Ẇshaft; ηP not meaningful for a turboshaft alone
Narration

Two architectures from the manual deserve a short mention. In a mixed-exhaust turbofan, the core and bypass streams join in a mixer and leave through one nozzle, the standard layout of military engines with afterburners. Because the mixer requires matching total pressures, the turbine pressure ratio is fixed first, and the power balance then delivers the bypass ratio: alpha becomes an output, the single structural difference from the separate-exhaust engine. Mixing recovers thrust by removing the velocity mismatch between the streams, a few per cent, but on a large civil engine the full-length duct costs more in weight and drag than it returns. The turboshaft splits the turbine: the high pressure turbine drives the compressor, and a free turbine drives the output shaft, judged by its power specific fuel consumption; its propulsive efficiency only makes sense together with its rotor or propeller.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: On-Design Aircraft Engine Analysis (14 Oct 2026), §6.2, §7

13. Momentum theory of the actuator disc

4 · Propellers
  • vp = (v0 + ve)/2: half the acceleration ahead, half behind
  • a = (vp − v0)/v0; F = 2ṁ a v0; ṁ = ρ0 AD vp
  • ẆD = F vp = ΔEk of the slipstream
  • Froude: ηP = F v0/ẆD = 1/(1 + a)
  • vp = [v0 + √(v0² + 2F/(ρ0 AD))]/2: disc loading F/AD sets the loss

Equations

vp​=2v0​+ve​​,a=v0​vp​−v0​​,F=2m˙av0​,m˙=ρ0​AD​vp​
W˙D​=Fvp​,ηP​=W˙D​Fv0​​=1+a1​,vp​=2v0​+v02​+2F/(ρ0​AD​)​​
Narration

A propeller produces thrust by giving a small velocity increase to a large mass of air, the opposite of a turbojet, and momentum theory captures it with an actuator disc. Bernoulli on each side of the disc and the momentum balance give the central result: the velocity at the disc is the average of the upstream and far-downstream velocities. With the axial induction factor a, the disc velocity is v zero times one plus a, the wake velocity v zero times one plus two a, and the thrust two m dot a v zero. The disc power, thrust times disc velocity, equals the kinetic energy given to the slipstream, and the ideal, Froude, propulsive efficiency is one over one plus a. Solving the quadratic for the disc velocity shows that the loss is governed by the disc loading, thrust over disc area: a big diameter means high efficiency, but also high tip speeds, ground clearance problems and root stresses.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: On-Design Aircraft Engine Analysis (14 Oct 2026), §8.1

14. Real propellers: ηprop = ηL · ηP

4 · Propellers
  • Shaft power → (ηL: profile drag, swirl, tips) → ẆD → (ηP = 1/(1+a)) → F v0
  • Cycle model: ηprop = 10 M0 ηmax (M0 ≤ 0.1), ηmax (≤ 0.7), ηmax[1 − (M0 − 0.7)/3] (≤ 0.85)
  • Fprop = ηprop Ẇprop / v0 → vp from AD → a, ẆD, ηL = ηprop(1+a) ≤ 1 (check!)
  • Static: F = (2ρ0AD)^(1/3) (FM·Ẇprop)^(2/3), FM ≈ 0.70–0.80; model limit F = 10 ηmax Ẇ/a0

Equations

Narration

A real propeller absorbs more shaft power than the ideal disc for the same thrust, because of profile drag, swirl and tip losses, collected in a loss efficiency eta L, so the propeller efficiency is eta L times eta P. For cycle work the course uses the algebraic model plotted here: ten M zero eta max at very low speed, constant at eta max up to Mach 0.7, and falling beyond, when the blade tips approach drag divergence. The procedure is: thrust from the shaft power, disc velocity from the disc area, then the induction factor, the disc power and eta L, which must not exceed one; otherwise the disc is too small for the assumed efficiency. At zero speed the efficiency is meaningless, so the static thrust uses the figure of merit, F equals two rho A to the one third times F M times the power to the two thirds, with F M around 0.7 to 0.8, while the model's own limit, ten eta max times the power over the speed of sound, can violate momentum theory, as exercise 1 B will show.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: On-Design Aircraft Engine Analysis (14 Oct 2026), §8.2, §9.4, §9.7

15. Turboprop analysis

4 · Propellers
  • Streams: ṁ0 = ṁjet + ṁprop, β = ṁprop/ṁjet is an OUTPUT of momentum theory
  • Ẇprop = ηg (ηm,L ẆLPT − ẆLPC): gearbox and shaft losses
  • F = Fjet + Fprop; TSFC = ṁf/F; PSFC = ṁf/Ẇprop
  • ΔEk = ½ṁ9v9² − ½ṁjet v0² + ẆD (disc power, not shaft power)
  • Optimum τtL: more expansion feeds the efficient propeller until the jet is starved; rises with M0

Narration

A turboprop is a turboshaft whose free turbine drives a propeller through a gearbox. It handles two streams, the gas generator flow and the propeller flow, and their ratio beta behaves like a bypass ratio, but it is an output of momentum theory, not a design choice, because the propeller has no duct. The power reaching the propeller is the gearbox efficiency times the shaft's share of the low pressure turbine power minus the low pressure compressor power. The total thrust adds the jet and the propeller, and the kinetic energy increment uses the disc power, so propeller losses appear in the thermal efficiency; Mattingly uses the shaft power instead, which gives a higher thermal and a lower propulsive efficiency but the same overall efficiency. Finally, there is an optimum free-turbine expansion: more expansion sends power to the efficient propeller until the jet is starved, and the optimum shifts towards the jet as the flight speed grows.

Sources: Miró, González, Soria: On-Design Aircraft Engine Analysis (14 Oct 2026), §9
