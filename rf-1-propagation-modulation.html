3. The radio spectrum and the aviation bands

1 · Spectrum
  • ITU bands are decades of frequency: LF 30–300 kHz, MF 0.3–3 MHz, HF 3–30 MHz, VHF 30–300 MHz, UHF 0.3–3 GHz, SHF 3–30 GHz
  • Aviation: NDB in LF/MF, HF voice for oceanic flights, VHF voice 118–137 MHz, VOR/ILS 108–118 MHz, DME/SSR/GPS in L band, radars in SHF
Narration

The electromagnetic spectrum used for radio is divided by the I T U into bands, each one a decade of frequency. Low frequency goes from 30 to 300 kHz, medium frequency up to 3 MHz, high frequency up to 30 MHz, very high frequency up to 300 MHz, ultra high frequency up to 3 GHz and super high frequency up to 30 GHz. Aviation uses almost all of them: non-directional beacons sit between 190 and 535 kHz, long-range voice over the oceans uses HF, between 2 and 30 MHz, and the familiar VHF voice band runs from 118 to 137 MHz. Just below it, from 108 to 118 MHz, are the VOR and the ILS localizer, with the glide slope near 330 MHz. Around 1 GHz, the L band holds the DME, the secondary radar at 1030 and 1090 MHz and GPS at 1575 MHz, and higher up come the radio altimeter at 4.3 GHz and the weather radar near 9.4 GHz.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 3–4 (aviation radio frequency bands table)

Equations

Narration

The first formula of the course links wavelength, frequency and the medium. The wavelength is the speed of light divided by the frequency and by the square root of the effective permittivity, because waves slow down in a dielectric. In air the permittivity is one, so 3 times ten to the 8 over ten to the 8 gives 3.00 m. In a polyethylene cable, with a permittivity of 2.3, the square root is 1.517, and the wavelength shrinks to 1.98 m: this is why a quarter-wave cable section is shorter than a quarter-wave antenna. And in water, with a permittivity of 80.4, the wavelength is only 0.335 m.

5. The atmosphere and the ionosphere

2 · Propagation
  • Troposphere: weather; refraction bends VHF slightly beyond the optical horizon
  • Ionosphere: ionised by the Sun, so it changes between day and night
  • D layer (day only) ABSORBS low frequencies; E, F1, F2 REFLECT up to ≈ 5, 10, 30 MHz
  • At night D disappears and the layers weaken: lower usable frequencies
Narration

Radio waves travel through an atmosphere with distinct layers. The troposphere, from the ground to about 10 to 12 km, is where the weather is, and it bends VHF waves slightly, extending their range a little beyond the optical horizon. Above the stratosphere, from 50 to 80 km up to 400 or 500 km, the ionosphere is ionised by solar radiation, so its properties change between day and night and with solar activity. The D layer, present only in daylight, absorbs the lower frequencies below about 4 MHz, while the E, F1 and F2 layers reflect waves up to about 5, 10 and 30 MHz respectively. At night the D layer disappears and the other layers weaken, which is why the usable frequencies drop at night.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slide 7 [Rueda]

6. Ground, space and sky waves

2 · Propagation
  • Ground (surface) wave: 20 kHz–50 MHz, follows the Earth; less loss over sea; vertical polarisation
  • Space (direct) wave: f > 50 MHz, line of sight, plus a ground-reflected ray
  • Sky wave: HF reflected by the ionosphere, can hop thousands of km
  • Tropospheric waves: scattering and ducting in the lower atmosphere
Narration

There are four ways for a radio wave to get from the transmitter to the receiver. The ground or surface wave follows the curvature of the Earth, from about 20 kHz up to 50 MHz; its losses grow with frequency and are larger over land than over sea, and they are smallest with vertical polarisation, which is why beacon antennas are vertical. Above about 50 MHz the ground wave dies quickly and communication relies on the space wave: a direct, line-of-sight ray, plus a ray reflected from the ground. In the HF band, the ionosphere can reflect the sky wave back to Earth, so a single hop can cover thousands of kilometres, which is how oceanic HF voice works. Finally, tropospheric waves are scattered or ducted in the lower atmosphere, occasionally carrying VHF far beyond the horizon.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 8–9, 11, 13 [Tooley, Binns]

8. Space waves and the radio horizon

2 · Propagation
  • VHF/UHF: range limited by the radio horizon (line of sight)
  • d [nmi] = 1.23 (√H_T + √H_R), heights in FEET
  • Range grows only with the square root of altitude: 4× higher → 2× farther
  • Units trap: convert metres to feet (1 ft = 0.3048 m) and the result is in nautical miles

Equations

d[nmi]=1.23(HT​[ft]​+HR​[ft]​)
heights in feet, distance in nautical miles
Narration

Above 50 MHz, the range is set by the radio horizon. VHF voice, VOR and DME all need line of sight between the antennas, so the curvature of the Earth limits the distance. Binns gives the practical formula: the range in nautical miles is 1.23 times the sum of the square roots of the two antenna heights in feet. Because of the square roots, the range grows slowly: flying four times higher only doubles the range. The trap is in the units: the heights must be in feet and the result is in nautical miles, so convert metres first, dividing by 0.3048.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slide 11 [Binns]

10. Sky waves: critical frequency and MUF

2 · Propagation
  • Critical frequency (vertical incidence): fc = 9√N (fc in Hz, N in electrons/m³)
  • Same formula in handy units: fc [MHz] = 9·10⁻³ √N [e⁻/cm³]
  • Oblique path at elevation θ: MUF ≈ fc / sin θ (secant law)
  • MUF: highest frequency that is reflected; LUF: lowest usable, set by D-layer absorption
  • MUF_day ≈ 2 MUF_night; optimum working frequency OWF = 0.85 MUF

Equations

fc​[Hz]=9N[e−/m3]​,fc​[MHz]=9×10−3N[e−/cm3]​
MUF=sinθfc​​,OWF=0.85MUF,MUFday​≈2MUFnight​
Narration

For sky waves, the key quantity is the electron density of the ionosphere. A wave sent straight up is reflected only if its frequency is below the critical frequency, fc, equal to 9 times the square root of the electron density, with fc in hertz and the density in electrons per cubic metre. Last year's solution used the same formula in friendlier units: fc in megahertz equals 9 times ten to the minus 3 times the square root of the density in electrons per cubic centimetre; both give the same number. A wave that hits the layer obliquely, leaving the ground at an elevation angle theta, can be reflected up to a higher frequency, the maximum usable frequency, approximately fc divided by the sine of theta. Below the MUF there is also a lowest usable frequency, the LUF, set by absorption in the D layer, so the usable window lies between the two. The MUF by day is roughly twice the MUF by night, and in practice one works at the optimum working frequency, 85 % of the MUF, for a safety margin.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 13–15 [Binns]; Solved exam 1 shared by classmates (compartit: solucio_parcial, Parcial NOENUNCIAT) (problem 3)

13. Reflection, refraction, diffraction, scattering

2 · Propagation
  • Reflection: obstacle much LARGER than λ (ground, buildings, aircraft skin)
  • Refraction: wave enters a medium with a different index and bends
  • Diffraction: wave meets an EDGE and bends round it (coverage behind hills)
  • Scattering: obstacles SMALLER than λ re-radiate in all directions (rain, foliage)
Narration

Obstacles and discontinuities modify propagation in four ways, and the size of the obstacle relative to the wavelength decides which. Reflection happens when a wave meets a flat object much larger than the wavelength, such as the ground or a hangar. Refraction happens when a wave passes from one medium into another and changes direction, as in the troposphere and the ionosphere. Diffraction happens when a wave meets an edge and bends around it, following the profile of the discontinuity, which gives some coverage behind hills. Scattering happens when the obstacles are smaller than the wavelength, like raindrops for radar waves, which fragment and re-radiate the wave in all directions.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 17–18

14. Atmospheric absorption and 'secure' bands

2 · Propagation
  • Clear-air absorption has peaks: H₂O at 22 and 183 GHz, O₂ at 60 and 119 GHz
  • Windows between them (35, 94 GHz) are used for long-range mm-wave links and radar
  • Secure, short-range links use an absorption PEAK: around 60 GHz the signal dies within a few km, so it cannot be intercepted far away
Narration

The slides finish propagation with a graph of atmospheric absorption against frequency, and a question: which bands would you choose for secure applications? The absorption has peaks where water vapour and oxygen molecules resonate: water at 22 and 183 GHz, and oxygen at 60 and 119 GHz. Between the peaks there are windows, such as 35 and 94 GHz, where millimetre-wave links and radars can reach far. For a secure link you want the opposite: work at an absorption peak, especially the 60 GHz oxygen line, where the signal dies out within a few kilometres, so an eavesdropper far away cannot receive it.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slide 19 (curve redrawn schematically)

15. The communication system

3 · Transmitters and receivers
  • Transmitter: generates the signal; receiver: recovers the wanted signal among interference and noise; transceiver: both
  • Baseband (BB): spectrum concentrated near f = 0; modulation moves it to a band-pass channel around f_C
  • Receiver chain: LNA → amplifiers → downconversion → demodulator (the first stage sets the noise, see part 2)
Narration

Now the systems that send and receive the waves. A transmitter is the system that generates the signal, a receiver recovers the wanted signal from a wide spectrum full of other transmitters, interference and noise, and a transceiver is both in one box. The information starts as a baseband signal, whose spectrum is concentrated near zero frequency; the modulator converts it into a band-pass signal around the carrier, which is then upconverted and amplified by the power amplifier. At the other end, a low-noise amplifier, more amplifiers, downconversion and a demodulator recover the baseband signal with as little noise and distortion as possible; we will see in part two why the first amplifier matters most.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 20–21

16. Amplitude modulation

4 · Modulation
  • x_MOD(t) = A(t) cos[ω_C t + φ(t)]: AM varies A, FM/PM vary φ
  • AM: x(t) = A_C[1 + m x(t)] cos(2π f_C t), m = A_m / A_C, 0 < m < 1
  • Spectrum: carrier + LSB + USB; DSB bandwidth = 2 f_ms
  • Ex.: audio 20 Hz–30 kHz on 100 kHz: DSB = 60 kHz, each sideband 29.98 kHz wide

Equations

xAM​(t)=AC​[1+mx(t)]cos(2πfC​t),m=AC​Am​​, 0<m<1
AC​cosωC​t+2mAC​​cos(ωC​−ωm​)t+2mAC​​cos(ωC​+ωm​)t,BDSB​=2fm​
tone message: carrier plus two sidebands
Narration

Any modulated carrier can be written as an amplitude A of t times the cosine of omega C t plus a phase phi of t: amplitude modulation varies A, frequency and phase modulation vary phi. In AM the envelope is the carrier amplitude times one plus m times the message, where the modulation index m is the message amplitude over the carrier amplitude, between zero and one; watch the envelope deepen as m grows. In the spectrum, the carrier is accompanied by a lower and an upper sideband, mirror images of the message, so double-sideband AM occupies twice the highest message frequency. In the slide example, an audio signal from 20 Hz to 30 kHz on a 100 kHz carrier needs 60 kHz, and each sideband is 29.98 kHz wide: the lower one runs from 70 to 99.98 kHz and the upper one from 100.02 to 130 kHz.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 23–26

17. Frequency modulation and Carson's rule

4 · Modulation
  • x_FM(t) = A_C cos[2π f_C t + φ(t)], φ(t) = k_f ∫ x dt: constant amplitude
  • Deviation Δf; modulation index m_f = Δf / f_m
  • Carson's rule (98 % of the power): B_FM = 2(Δf + f_m) = 2 f_m (m_f + 1)
  • Line spectrum at f_C ± n f_m with Bessel amplitudes J_n(m_f)

Equations

xFM​(t)=AC​cos[2πfC​t+kf​∫x(τ)dτ],mf​=fm​Δf​
xFM​(t)=AC​n=−∞∑∞​Jn​(mf​)cos(2π(fC​+nfm​)t),BFM​=2(Δf+fm​)=2fm​(mf​+1)
Bessel line spectrum and Carson's rule (≈ 98 % of the power)
Narration

In frequency modulation the amplitude stays constant and the phase is the integral of the message, so the instantaneous frequency follows the message. The peak frequency excursion is the deviation, delta f, and the modulation index m f is the deviation divided by the modulating frequency. The bandwidth that contains about 98 % of the power is given by Carson's rule: twice the sum of the deviation and the modulating frequency, or twice f m times m f plus one. The spectrum is a set of lines spaced f m apart around the carrier, with amplitudes given by Bessel functions of the index, and Carson's band, shaded, contains the significant ones.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 27–28 [Binns, Rueda]

19. Digital modulation: ASK, FSK, PSK

4 · Modulation
  • ASK: '1' → carrier, '0' → 0 V
  • FSK: '1' → f₁, '0' → f₂
  • PSK: a '0' shifts the phase 180°
  • Modulators are switches or multipliers; demodulators: envelope detector (ASK), two filters (FSK), coherent multiplication (PSK)
Narration

With a digital message the three parameters of the carrier give three basic keyings. In amplitude shift keying a one sends the carrier and a zero sends nothing. In frequency shift keying a one and a zero are sent at two different frequencies. In phase shift keying the phase jumps by 180 degrees between the two symbols. The modulators on the slides are simple switches or multipliers, and the demodulators are an envelope detector for ASK, a pair of filters for FSK and a coherent multiplication with the carrier for PSK.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 30–33

20. Sharing the medium: FDM and TDM

4 · Modulation
  • Band: whole spectrum of a standard; channel: bandwidth of ONE user
  • FDM: each user gets a frequency slot (carriers spaced by channel + guard band)
  • TDM: each user gets a time slot on the whole band
  • Slide problem (SSB, upper sideband): carriers 700, 710, … 790 MHz, each channel fc → fc + 6 MHz + 4 MHz guard; 700–800 MHz, × 10 GHz LO → 10.7–10.8 GHz (DSB: 12 + 4 = 16 MHz spacing)
Narration

A band is the whole range of spectrum a standard may use, a channel is the bandwidth of a single user, and multiplexing lets several signals share one medium without interfering. In frequency-division multiplexing each user is given its own frequency slot, with guard bands between them so that real filters can separate them. In time-division multiplexing users take turns, each one using the whole band during its time slot. In the slide problem each signal carries 6 MHz of video and needs a 4 MHz guard band, so each channel occupies a 10 MHz slot, but only if each channel is single-sideband. With the upper sideband, a carrier at 700 MHz puts the video from 700 to 706 MHz, the guard band takes it to 710, the next carrier is at 710, and so on up to 790 MHz; the slide's figure marks its carriers at the slot centres, 705, 715 and so on, which is only schematic, although for a hand sketch 6 MHz blocks centred on those carriers, 702 to 708 MHz with 4 MHz guards, match the professor's own figure. With double sideband each channel would occupy 12 MHz and need a spacing of 16 MHz, so state your assumption. Either way the ten channels fill 700 to 800 MHz, and the 10 GHz upconverter moves the multiplex to 10.7 to 10.8 GHz.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 34–36 (FDM example figure)

21. Transmitters: direct conversion and I/Q

5 · Architectures
  • Direct conversion: f_C = f_LO; modulation and upconversion in one circuit
  • I/Q: the bit stream is split into PAIRS; I and Q modulate cos and sin of the same carrier
  • Two-step transmitter: modulate at an IF, then upconvert (easier filtering)
  • QAM: two carriers 90° apart, can implement AM, FM or digital constellations
Narration

In a direct-conversion transmitter the carrier frequency equals the local-oscillator frequency, and modulation and upconversion happen in the same circuit. It is usually built with in-phase and quadrature branches: the bit stream is divided into pairs of bits, one modulating a cosine and the other a sine of the same carrier, which increases robustness and spectral efficiency. A two-step transmitter instead modulates at an intermediate frequency and then upconverts, which makes filtering easier. This quadrature idea is quadrature amplitude modulation, QAM: two carriers 90 degrees apart, and with the right baseband signals it can generate AM, FM or digital constellations.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 37–39

22. The superheterodyne receiver

5 · Architectures
  • Mixer: f_IF = |f_RF − f_LO|; tune by moving the LO, filter at a FIXED IF
  • Selective, high-gain IF stages → good sensitivity and selectivity
  • Image frequency f_im = f_LO ± f_IF (other side of the LO) also lands on the IF
  • The RF filter before the mixer must reject the image

Equations

fIF​=∣fRF​−fLO​∣,fim​=fLO​±fIF​  (high-side LO: fim​=fRF​+2fIF​)
Narration

The workhorse receiver is the superheterodyne: a mixer multiplies the incoming signal by a local oscillator and produces the difference frequency, the intermediate frequency. Tuning is done by moving the local oscillator, while all the selective, high-gain filtering is done at a fixed IF, which gives excellent sensitivity and selectivity; the price is more circuitry and power consumption. Its weakness is the image: a signal on the other side of the local oscillator, at the same distance, also produces the IF. So the RF filter before the mixer must reject the image frequency, which is why the slides list the problem of the image among the design issues.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 40–41 [Binns]

In last year's exam the receiver question was short. The architecture is the superheterodyne, which converts every channel to the same fixed IF of 455 kHz. With the local oscillator above the signal, its frequency is 5.5 plus 0.455, which is 5.955 MHz, the answer in the solved copy.
