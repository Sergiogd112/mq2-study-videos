Equations

G[dB]=10logP1​P2​​,P[dBm]=P[dBW]+30

Narration

Radio engineers work in decibels because gains multiply and signal levels span many orders of magnitude. A power ratio in decibels is 10 times the logarithm of the ratio, so doubling the power adds 3.01 dB and multiplying by ten adds 10 dB. An absolute power needs a reference: dBW is relative to one watt, and dBm to one milliwatt, so the dBm value is always the dBW value plus 30. Voltages use 20 times the logarithm, because power goes with the square of the voltage, and the simple formula holds only when both voltages are across equal impedances. Voltage levels are given in dBV, relative to one volt, or dBµV, relative to one microvolt, and the dBµV value is the dBV value plus 120. Keep the rule in mind: 10 log for powers, 20 log for voltages, and a plain dB is always a ratio, never a level.

4. Calculating with decibels

  1. rules: dB + dB = dB; dBm + dB = dBm; dBm + dBm: NO, add milliwatts
  2. (a) G=200/1=200⇒10log200=23.01 dB
  3. (b) L=1/0.5=2⇒3.01 dB of loss (G=−3.01 dB)
  4. (c) 1+1.995+0.251=3.246 mW ⇒10log3.246=5.11 dBm

Narration

Decibels make cascades easy, but there is one classic trap. Gains in dB add, and a level in dBm plus a gain in dB gives a level in dBm, so 30 dBm through a 10 dB amplifier is 40 dBm; but two levels in dBm cannot be added: 30 dBm plus 30 dBm is one watt plus one watt, two watts, which is 33 dBm, not 60. For part a, 200 milliwatts out for one milliwatt in is a gain of 200, which is 23.01 dB. For part b, the attenuator halves the power, a loss of 2, which is 3.01 dB, or a gain of minus 3.01 dB. For part c, convert each level to milliwatts: one, plus 1.995, plus 0.251, which is 3.246 milliwatts, and ten log of that is 5.11 dBm: barely more than the largest term, as always when adding powers. And for part d, the gains in dB simply add, 3 times 23 minus 3 is 66 dB, a linear gain of almost four million.

5. Noise sources and kTB

2 · Noise
  • Thermal noise: random motion of charges in any resistor at T > 0 K; V_n,rms = √(4 k T B R)
  • k = 1.38·10⁻²³ J/K; e.g. R = 50 Ω, T = 290 K, B = 1 MHz → V_n = 0.89 µV
  • Available noise power into a matched load: N = k T B (independent of R)
  • At T₀ = 290 K: k T₀ = 4.0·10⁻²¹ W/Hz = −204 dBW/Hz = −174 dBm/Hz
  • Also: shot noise (discrete electrons) and flicker noise (N ∝ 1/f, at low frequency)

Equations

Vn,rms​=4kTBR​,N=kTB,k=1.38×10−23 J/K
kT0​=4.0×10−21 W/Hz=−204 dBW/Hz=−174 dBm/Hz(T0​=290 K)
Narration

Noise sets the smallest signal a receiver can detect, so it limits the range of every radio link. The basic source is thermal noise: in any resistor above absolute zero, the random agitation of charges produces a noise voltage whose r m s value is the square root of 4 k T B R. With Boltzmann's constant, 1.38 times ten to the minus 23 joules per kelvin, a 50 ohm resistor at 290 kelvin in a 1 megahertz bandwidth gives 0.89 microvolts. The power that this resistor delivers to a matched load is simply k T B, which does not depend on the resistance at all. At the reference temperature T zero of 290 kelvin, k T zero is 4 times ten to the minus 21 watts per hertz, which is minus 204 dBW per hertz, or minus 174 dBm per hertz: memorise this number, it appears in every noise problem. Devices add other noise as well: shot noise from the discrete nature of the current, and flicker noise, which grows as one over the frequency and matters mostly at low frequencies.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 47, 60

6. SNR, noise figure and noise temperature

2 · Noise
  • SNR = S / N; SNR [dB] = 10 log (S / N)
  • Noise figure: F = SNR_in / SNR_out ≥ 1; NF [dB] = SNR_in [dB] − SNR_out [dB]
  • Defined with a matched input noise source at T₀ = 290 K
  • Output noise: N_o = k B G (T₀ + T_e), so F = 1 + T_e / T₀ and T_e = T₀ (F − 1)
  • F is used LINEAR in formulas, NF in dB on datasheets (careful which is which)

Equations

F=SNRout​SNRin​​≥1,NF[dB]=10logF
No​=kBG(T0​+Te​),F=1+T0​Te​​,Te​=T0​(F−1)
Narration

The quality of the received signal is measured by the signal-to-noise ratio, the signal power over the noise power, usually in decibels. Every real component adds noise of its own, so the SNR gets worse from input to output, and the noise figure measures by how much: the input SNR over the output SNR, at least one, or in decibels the input SNR minus the output SNR. It is defined with a matched noise source at the reference temperature of 290 kelvin at the input. Equivalently, the component can be described by its equivalent noise temperature T e: the output noise is k B G times T zero plus T e, so the noise factor is one plus T e over T zero, and T e is T zero times F minus one. A practical warning: the formulas use the linear factor F, while datasheets give the noise figure in decibels, so always convert before using the cascade formula.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 48–49

7. Cascaded stages: the Friis formula

2 · Noise
  • F_T = F₁ + (F₂ − 1)/G₁ + (F₃ − 1)/(G₁G₂) + … (all LINEAR)
  • Each stage's excess noise is divided by the gain BEFORE it
  • So the first stage usually dominates (if its gain is high): put a low-noise, high-gain LNA first
  • A passive lossy part (filter, cable, passive mixer) at T₀: G = 1/L and F = L
  • Gain: G_T [dB] = ΣG_i [dB] − ΣL_j [dB]

Equations

FT​=F1​+G1​F2​−1​+G1​G2​F3​−1​+⋯
all in linear units
G=L1​,F=L
passive lossy part at T₀ with loss L
Narration

When several stages are cascaded, their noise combines according to the Friis formula. The total noise factor is the first factor, plus the second factor minus one divided by the first gain, plus the third factor minus one divided by the product of the first two gains, and so on, all in linear units. Each stage's own noise is divided by the gain in front of it, because the signal has already been amplified when it gets there. So, provided its gain is high, the first stage dominates, and that is why every receiver starts with a low-noise amplifier with a high gain: the stages after it then hardly matter; with a lossy or low-gain first stage, compare the terms instead. A passive lossy component, such as a filter, a cable or a passive mixer at room temperature, has a gain of one over its loss and a noise factor equal to its loss, which is why a lossy cable in front of the LNA is so harmful. And the total gain in dB is just the sum of the gains minus the sum of the losses.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 46, 50; Pozar, Microwave Engineering (lossy line noise figure F = L at T₀)

10. Noise floor and sensitivity in decibels

2 · Noise
  • N_o [dBm] = −174 + NF + 10 log B + G
  • Referred to the input (G = 0): k T₀ B F
  • Sensitivity: P_min [dBm] = −174 + NF + 10 log B + SNR_min
  • Example (bars): B = 1 MHz, NF = 5 dB, SNR_min = 10 dB → P_min = −99 dBm
  • Bandwidth costs 10 dB per decade: narrow filters improve sensitivity

Equations

No​[dBm]=−174+NF+10logB+G
Pmin​[dBm]=−174+NF+10logB+SNRmin​
Narration

In decibels the noise calculations become a sum, which the bar chart builds step by step. Start from minus 174 dBm per hertz, add ten log of the bandwidth, add the noise figure, and add the gain if you want the noise at the output: that is the noise floor. Without the gain, the same sum is the noise referred to the input, k T zero B F. The sensitivity, the weakest signal that still gives the required output SNR, is the input noise plus that minimum SNR. With a 1 megahertz bandwidth, a 5 dB noise figure and a 10 dB minimum SNR, the bars give minus 174 plus 60 plus 5 plus 10, which is minus 99 dBm. Notice that each decade of bandwidth costs 10 dB, so a narrow-band receiver is much more sensitive than a wide-band one.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 60, 62

11. Non-linearity: harmonics and intermodulation

3 · Linearity
  • v_o = α₀ + α₁v_i + α₂v_i² + α₃v_i³ + …
  • One tone A cos ω₀t → DC, ω₀, 2ω₀, 3ω₀ (harmonics, easy to filter)
  • α₃ < 0: fundamental gain α₁ + ¾α₃A² falls → gain compression
  • Two tones ω₁, ω₂ → IM3 at 2ω₁ − ω₂ and 2ω₂ − ω₁: IN band, cannot be filtered

Equations

vo​=α0​+α1​vi​+α2​vi2​+α3​vi3​+⋯
Gfund​=α1​+43​α3​A2
one tone A cos ω₀t: the fundamental gain compresses when α₃ < 0
Narration

Every real amplifier is non-linear, and we model its output as a power series of the input: alpha zero plus alpha one times the input plus alpha two times its square plus alpha three times its cube. With one tone at the input, the squared and cubed terms generate a DC term and harmonics at twice and three times the frequency; they are far from the signal, so a filter removes them. The cubic term also changes the fundamental: its gain becomes alpha one plus three quarters alpha three A squared, and since alpha three is negative in practical amplifiers, the gain falls as the amplitude grows, which is gain compression. The real problem appears with two close tones: the cubic term produces third-order intermodulation products at twice omega one minus omega two and twice omega two minus omega one, right next to the wanted signals, in red, where no filter can remove them.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 53–55

12. 1 dB compression point and third-order intercept

3 · Linearity
  • 1 dB CP: output 1 dB below the ideal line
  • IM3 grows 3 dB per dB of input (slope 3)
  • IP3: where the two lines would cross; IIP3 at input, OIP3 at output
  • OIP3 = IIP3 + G; 1 dB CP ≈ IIP3 − 9.6 dB
  • IM3 level: P_IM3,in = 3P_in − 2 IIP3 → C/IM3 = 2 (IIP3 − P_in)

Equations

OIP3=IIP3+G,P1dB​≈IIP3−9.6 dB
PIM3,in​=3Pin​−2IIP3,IM3C​=2(IIP3−Pin​)
third-order product referred to the input
Narration

On a plot of output power against input power, both in dBm, the fundamental is a straight line of slope one until it starts to compress; the 1 dB compression point is where it falls 1 dB below the ideal line. The third-order products grow with the cube of the input, so on the decibel plot their line has a slope of 3: every extra dB of input adds 3 dB of intermodulation. Extending both straight lines, they meet at the third-order intercept point; its input coordinate is the IIP3, and its output coordinate the OIP3. The two are related by the gain, OIP3 equals IIP3 plus G, and for a cubic non-linearity the 1 dB compression point lies about 9.6 dB below the IIP3. A useful extra: the input-referred intermodulation level is 3 times the input power minus twice the IIP3, so the carrier-to-intermodulation ratio is twice the distance between the IIP3 and the input power.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 54–56; Pozar, Microwave Engineering ch. 10 (IM3 level)

Narration

For linearity the cascade formula works the other way round from Friis: the reciprocal of the total IIP3 is the sum of each stage's reciprocal IIP3, multiplied by the gain in front of it, so a stage weighs more the more gain sits in front of it and the lower its own IIP3: later stages are often the bottleneck, but not always. Convert to linear: gains of 1.585, 0.316 and 100, and intercepts of 10, 39.8 and 100 milliwatts. The sum is one over 10, plus 1.585 over 39.8, plus 1.585 times 0.316 over 100, which is 0.1448 per milliwatt; here the first stage contributes 69 %, the second 27 % and the last only 3.5 %, because the attenuator in front of it cuts its gain, so compare the terms instead of trusting a rule. Its inverse is 6.91 milliwatts, 8.4 dBm. The total gain is 2 minus 5 plus 20, 17 dB, so the OIP3 is 25.4 dBm. And the 1 dB compression point is about 9.6 dB below the IIP3, minus 1.2 dBm at the input.

15. Exam problem 7: cascade IIP3 (reconstructed)

3 · Linearity
Problem. Last year's problem 7 (problem 1 of the other solved copy, 'ADS system'): LNA (G 20 dB, NF 2 dB, OIP3 10 dBm), mixer (G 15 dB, NF 6 dB, OIP3 5 dBm), third block (NF 2.5 dB, IIP3 100 dBm, gain hidden on the copy); P_in = −40 dBm, 474.25 MHz. Find the system IIP3.
  1. IIP3 = OIP3 − G: LNA 10−20=−10 dBm; mixer 5−15=−10 dBm
  2. 1/IIP3T​=0.11​+0.1100​+10103162​=1010 mW−1
  3. IIP3T​=0.99μW =−30.0 dBm; OIP3 =−30+32.5=2.5 dBm
  4. C/IM3 =2(−30+40)=20 dB; FT​=1.615, NFT​=2.08 dB

Narration

Last year's exam had a cascade intercept question: one solved copy crossed it out, and the other, where it is problem 1, got it wrong, so let us do it properly; the third block's gain is hidden on the copy, but it hardly matters. The datasheet values are output intercepts, so first subtract each stage's gain: the LNA has an IIP3 of 10 minus 20, minus 10 dBm, and the mixer 5 minus 15, also minus 10 dBm. In milliwatts, the sum is one over 0.1, plus 100 over 0.1, plus a negligible term for the very linear filter, about 1 010 per milliwatt. So the system IIP3 is about one microwatt, minus 30 dBm, set almost entirely by the mixer, which sees the full 20 dB of LNA gain; assuming the third block is a 2.5 dB loss, the net gain is 32.5 dB and the OIP3 is 2.5 dBm. With the given input of minus 40 dBm, the intermodulation products sit twice 10, that is 20 dB, below the carriers, and since all the noise figures are given, Friis gives a total noise factor of 1.615, a noise figure of 2.08 dB. The classmate's copy put the output intercepts straight into the formula as input intercepts and got minus 15 dBm, which was marked incorrect: always convert OIP3 to IIP3 first.

16. Sensitivity: the weakest usable signal

4 · Sensitivity
  • F = SNR_in / SNR_out with SNR_out = SNR_min and N_in = k T₀ B
  • P_min = k T₀ · B · F · SNR_min
  • P_min [dBm] = −174 + NF [dB] + 10 log B + SNR_min [dB]
  • SNR_min comes from the demodulator: a BER curve for digital links
  • To improve P_min: lower NF (better LNA), narrower B, more robust modulation

Equations

Pmin​=kT0​BFSNRmin​⟹Pmin​[dBm]=−174+NF+10logB+SNRmin​
Narration

The sensitivity is the minimum input signal for which the receiver still delivers an acceptable output SNR. Start from the definition of the noise factor with the output SNR at its minimum allowed value and the input noise equal to k T zero B. Solving for the signal, the minimum power is k T zero, times the bandwidth, times the noise factor, times the minimum SNR. In decibels, the sensitivity in dBm is minus 174, plus the noise figure, plus ten log of the bandwidth, plus the minimum SNR. For a digital link the minimum SNR comes from a bit-error-rate curve: the SNR that gives the required BER. And the formula tells you how to improve a receiver: a better LNA, a narrower bandwidth, or a modulation that needs less SNR.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slide 60

18. Dynamic range

5 · Dynamic range
  • Ratio between the largest and smallest useful levels
  • Noise floor N_o = −174 + NF + 10 log B + G (typically −60 to −100 dBm)
  • Output: DR_o [dB] = 1dBCP_o [dBm] − N_o [dBm]
  • Input: DR_i [dB] = 1dBCP_i [dBm] − P_min [dBm]
  • 1dBCP_i = 1dBCP_o − (G − 1) (the gain is 1 dB compressed there)

Equations

DRo​=1dBCPo​−No​,DRi​=1dBCPi​−Pmin​,1dBCPi​=1dBCPo​−(G−1)
Narration

The dynamic range is the ratio between the largest and the smallest levels for which the system behaves as desired. The bottom is set by the noise floor, minus 174 plus the noise figure plus ten log of the bandwidth plus the gain, typically between minus 60 and minus 100 dBm over the system bandwidth. The top is set by compression, so the linear dynamic range at the output is the output 1 dB compression point minus the output noise floor. Referred to the input, it is the input 1 dB compression point minus the sensitivity. To move the compression point between input and output, remember that the gain there is already compressed by one dB, so the input point is the output point minus the gain plus one.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026), slides 62–63

22. Traps that cost points

Summary
  • Never add dBm to dBm: convert to mW, add, convert back
  • Friis and the IIP3 cascade need LINEAR values (F, G, mW)
  • Friis: the first stage dominates if its gain is high; IIP3: compare G_before / IIP3_i (often a late stage)
  • Passive loss L: G = −L dB and NF = L dB
  • OIP3 vs IIP3: subtract the gain before the cascade formula
  • 1dBCP_i = 1dBCP_o − G + 1; −174 dBm/Hz only at T₀ = 290 K
Narration

Before the summary, the traps that cost points last year. Never add levels in dBm: convert to milliwatts, add, and convert back. Both cascade formulas need linear values, noise factors, gains and intercepts in milliwatts. In the Friis formula the first stage dominates if its gain is high, while in the intercept cascade each stage weighs its preceding gain over its own IIP3, which often makes a late stage the bottleneck, but always compare the terms. A passive lossy stage has a gain of minus its loss and a noise figure equal to its loss. If a datasheet gives the output intercept, subtract the gain to get the input intercept before using the cascade formula. And remember that the input compression point is the output one minus the gain plus one, and that minus 174 dBm per hertz holds only at 290 kelvin; for another source temperature use k T B directly.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 1: Propagació d'ones de RF (2026); Solved exam 1 shared by classmates (compartit: solucio_parcial, Parcial NOENUNCIAT)
