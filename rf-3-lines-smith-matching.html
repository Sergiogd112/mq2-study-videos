2. When does a wire become a transmission line?

1 · Lines
  • Transmission line: medium where voltage and current VARY along its length
  • Guided wavelength λg = c / (f √εeff)
  • Lumped circuit if the length ≪ λg; transmission line if it is a sizeable fraction of λg (rule of thumb > λg/10)
  • Example: 1 m of PE coax (εr 2.3) is λg/2 at 99 MHz but only ≈ λg/4000 at 50 kHz

Equations

λg​=fεeff​​c​,transmission line if l≳10λg​​
Narration

A transmission line is a physical medium that carries electrical energy, in which the voltage and the current vary along its length. What matters is the length compared with the guided wavelength, the speed of light over f times the square root of the effective permittivity. If the line is much shorter than a wavelength, all points see the same voltage at the same time and ordinary circuit theory works; once it is a sizeable fraction of a wavelength, as a rule of thumb more than a tenth, we must treat it as a line with waves travelling along it. For example, one metre of polyethylene coaxial cable is half a wavelength long at 99 MHz, so it is a transmission line there, but at 50 kHz it is about a four-thousandth of a wavelength and behaves as a simple wire.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 2: Línies de transmissió i sistemes guiats (2026), slide 3

3. The RLGC model of a line

1 · Lines
  • R [Ω/m]: conductor losses; L [H/m]: inductance of the conductors
  • G [S/m]: dielectric losses; C [F/m]: capacitance between the conductors
  • Telegrapher's solution: V(x) = V⁺e^(−γx) + V⁻e^(γx), forward + reflected wave
  • γ = √((R + jωL)(G + jωC)) = α + jβ; Z₀ = V⁺/I⁺ = √((R + jωL)/(G + jωC))

Equations

V(x)=V+e−γx+V−eγx,γ=(R+jωL)(G+jωC)​=α+jβ
Z0​=I+V+​=G+jωCR+jωL​​
Narration

A short piece of line is modelled by four parameters per unit length: a series resistance R for the conductor losses and a series inductance L, both in the direction of the current. And a shunt conductance G for the losses in the dielectric, and a shunt capacitance C between the two conductors. Solving the circuit equations for a chain of these pieces, the voltage along the line is the sum of a forward wave, V plus times e to the minus gamma x, and a reflected wave, V minus times e to the plus gamma x; the current has the same two waves with a minus sign between them. Gamma, the propagation constant, is the square root of R plus j omega L times G plus j omega C, with real part alpha, the attenuation, and imaginary part beta, the phase constant; and the characteristic impedance Z zero, the ratio of voltage to current in a single wave, is the square root of R plus j omega L over G plus j omega C.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 2: Línies de transmissió i sistemes guiats (2026), slides 4–5

4. The lossless line

1 · Lines
  • R = G = 0: Z₀ = √(L / C) (real)
  • γ = jβ, β = ω√(LC) = 2π / λg
  • Phase velocity v_p = f λg = ω / β = 1 / √(LC)
  • V(x) = V⁺e^(−jβx) + V⁻e^(jβx); I(x) = (V⁺/Z₀) e^(−jβx) − (V⁻/Z₀) e^(jβx)
  • Useful: Z₀ = √(L/C) and v_p = 1/√(LC) give Z₀ = 1 / (v_p C) = L v_p

Equations

Z0​=CL​​,β=ωLC​=λg​2π​,vp​=LC​1​,Z0​=vp​C1​=Lvp​
Narration

In most problems the line is lossless, R and G are zero, and everything becomes simpler. The characteristic impedance is the square root of L over C, a real number such as 50 or 75 ohms. The propagation constant is purely imaginary, j beta, with beta equal to omega times the square root of L C, which is also two pi over the guided wavelength. The phase velocity is f times lambda g, omega over beta, which is one over the square root of L C. The waves travel without attenuation, only changing phase. Combining the two formulas gives a result we will use for coaxial cables: Z zero is one over the phase velocity times the capacitance per metre.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 2: Línies de transmissió i sistemes guiats (2026), slide 6

5. Reflection coefficient, input impedance and power

2 · Reflection
  • Γ(x) = V⁻e^(jβx) / V⁺e^(−jβx); at the load: Γ_L = (Z_L − Z₀)/(Z_L + Z₀)
  • Z_L = Z₀ → Γ_L = 0: no reflection (matched)
  • Z_in = Z₀ (Z_L + jZ₀ tan βl) / (Z₀ + jZ_L tan βl); periodic every λg/2
  • Along the line: Γ(−l) = Γ_L e^(−j2βl) (same modulus, phase turns)
  • P_r = |Γ_L|² P_in; P_t = (1 − |Γ_L|²) P_in

Equations

ΓL​=ZL​+Z0​ZL​−Z0​​,Γ(−l)=ΓL​e−j2βl
Zin​=Z0​Z0​+jZL​tanβlZL​+jZ0​tanβl​,Pr​=∣ΓL​∣2Pin​,Pt​=(1−∣ΓL​∣2)Pin​
Narration

When the line ends in a load different from Z zero, part of the wave is reflected. The reflection coefficient is the ratio of the reflected to the forward wave, and at the load it is Z L minus Z zero over Z L plus Z zero. If the load equals Z zero, the coefficient is zero and nothing is reflected: the load is matched. Looking into a line of length l terminated in Z L, the input impedance is Z zero times Z L plus j Z zero tan beta l, over Z zero plus j Z L tan beta l; since the tangent repeats every half wavelength, so does the input impedance. Moving back from the load towards the generator, the reflection coefficient keeps its modulus and its phase decreases by twice beta l, a fact that is the basis of the Smith chart. For power, the reflected power is the modulus of gamma squared times the incident power, and the load receives the rest, one minus gamma squared; maximum power is delivered when gamma is zero.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 2: Línies de transmissió i sistemes guiats (2026), slides 7–8

6. Standing waves and the SWR

2 · Reflection
  • Forward + reflected wave → standing wave
  • SWR = V_max / V_min = (1 + |Γ|) / (1 − |Γ|)
  • |Γ| = (SWR − 1) / (SWR + 1)
  • Matched: SWR = 1; short or open: SWR = ∞
  • Maxima and minima repeat every λg/2

Equations

SWR=Vmin​Vmax​​=1−∣Γ∣1+∣Γ∣​,∣Γ∣=SWR+1SWR−1​
Narration

The forward and reflected waves interfere and create a standing-wave pattern along the line; watch the three cases, with the load on the right. With a matched load there is only the forward wave, and the envelope of the voltage, in orange, is flat. With a partial reflection, here gamma equal to one half, the envelope swings between a maximum of one plus gamma and a minimum of one minus gamma. The voltage standing-wave ratio, SWR, is the maximum over the minimum, one plus the modulus of gamma over one minus the modulus of gamma; here 1.5 over 0.5, which is 3. Inverting it, the modulus of gamma is SWR minus one over SWR plus one, the formula you need in almost every exam question. With a short or an open circuit everything is reflected, the minima are true zeros and the SWR is infinite, while a matched line has an SWR of one; the maxima and minima repeat every half wavelength.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 2: Línies de transmissió i sistemes guiats (2026), slide 10

Equations

Narration

The slides ask you to discuss the special cases of the input impedance formula, and they are worth memorising. At low frequency beta l is almost zero, the tangent vanishes and the input impedance is just the load: the line is invisible. A matched line shows Z zero at its input whatever its length. A short-circuited line gives j Z zero tan beta l, and an open line minus j Z zero cotangent beta l: both are pure reactances, which is how stubs make inductors and capacitors. A half-wavelength line repeats the load exactly. And a quarter-wavelength line inverts it, Z zero squared over Z L, so a short becomes an open and an open becomes a short; we will use this in the quarter-wave transformer.

8. Exam problem 4: a reactive load at λ/8 + nλ/2

2 · Reflection
Problem. Last year's exam 1, problem 4 (as reconstructed from the solutions): a lossless line of length l = λ/8 + nλ/2 is terminated in Z_L = jZ₀. Find Z_in and explain what it means.
  1. βl=λ2π​(8λ​+2nλ​)=4π​+nπ⇒tanβl=1 for every n
  2. Zin​=Z0​Z0​+j(jZ0​)⋅1jZ0​+jZ0​⋅1​=Z0​Z0​−Z0​2jZ0​​=02jZ02​​

Narration

This question looks hard but is a one-liner once you see the trick. Beta l is two pi over lambda times an eighth of a wavelength plus n halves, which is pi over 4 plus n pi; the n pi does not change the tangent, so tan beta l is one for every n. Substituting, the numerator is j Z zero plus j Z zero, twice j Z zero, and the denominator is Z zero plus j times j Z zero, which is Z zero minus Z zero, zero. So the input impedance is infinite: the line looks like an open circuit, whatever n is. You can confirm it with the reflection coefficient: for Z L equal to j Z zero, gamma at the load is j minus one over j plus one, which is j, and moving an eighth of a wavelength towards the generator rotates it by 90 degrees clockwise, to plus one, the open-circuit point.

11. Return loss, insertion loss and the power budget

3 · Specifications
Problem. RL = −10 log(P_r/P_in) = −20 log|Γ_L|; IL = −10 log(P_t/P_in). Module 2 example: a 2-port has SWR = 1.5 over 2.5–5.5 GHz and IL = 0.5 dB. Calculate the percentage of P_r and P_t, and the RL.
  1. ∣ΓL​∣=1.5+11.5−1​=0.2⇒Pr​=0.22Pin​=4%
  2. Pt​=Pin​10−0.5/10=0.891Pin​=89%
  3. RL =−20log0.2=13.98 dB
  4. budget: 89 % out, 4 % reflected, 7 % lost (output mismatch + losses)
4 % reflected89 % transmitted13.98 dB return loss

Equations

RL=−10logPin​Pr​​=−20log∣ΓL​∣,IL=−10logPin​Pt​​
Narration

Datasheets of microwave components specify three things: the SWR over the operating band, the insertion loss, which is the loss in transmission, and the return loss, which measures the input mismatch; both losses are positive numbers in dB. For an SWR of 1.5, the modulus of gamma is 0.5 over 2.5, which is 0.2, so the reflected power is 0.2 squared, 4 % of the incident power. An insertion loss of 0.5 dB means the output power is ten to the minus 0.05 times the input, 0.891, so 89 % of the power gets through. The return loss is minus 20 log of 0.2, 13.98 dB. So of the input power, 89 % comes out, 4 % is reflected at the input, and the remaining 7 % is lost in the circuit and in the output mismatch.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 2: Línies de transmissió i sistemes guiats (2026), slides 13–14

Narration

The slides ask you to fill this table and judge the matching of a coaxial antenna system with a limit of 10 % reflected power. For each SWR, the modulus of gamma is SWR minus one over SWR plus one, the return loss is minus 20 log of it, and the reflected power is its square. An SWR of 1.2 reflects less than one percent, 1.5 reflects 4 %, 2 reflects 11 % and 3 reflects a quarter of the power. The 10 % limit corresponds to a gamma of the square root of 0.1, 0.316, an SWR of 1.92 and a return loss of 10 dB, so an SWR of 1.5 is a good match and an SWR of 2 is just outside the criterion.

14. Coaxial cables: Z₀, velocity factor, capacitance

4 · Coaxial cables
  • Z₀ = (138 / √εr) log₁₀(D / d) (D outer, d inner diameter)
  • Velocity factor VF = v_p / c = 1 / √εr (PE εr 2.3 → 66 %; PTFE 2.1 → 69 %)
  • C = 24.12 εr / log₁₀(D/d) pF/m = 3326 √εr / Z₀ pF/m
  • Equivalently Z₀ = 1 / (v_p C): datasheet VF and C give Z₀
  • LMR-400: VF 84 %, 78.4 pF/m → Z₀ = 1/(0.84·3·10⁸·78.4·10⁻¹²) = 50.6 Ω
  • Attenuation: A = 1.43 R/Z₀ + 9.15 √εr · f · F dB/100 m, R = 25.4 (1/d + 1/D) √f (d, D in mm; f in MHz; F = tan δ)

Equations

Z0​=εr​​138​log10​dD​,VF=cvp​​=εr​​1​
C=log10​(D/d)24.12εr​​ pF/m=Z0​3326εr​​​ pF/m,Z0​=vp​C1​
Narration

The coaxial cable is the line you will meet most in aircraft, from the radio to the antenna. Its characteristic impedance depends only on the ratio of the outer to the inner diameter and on the dielectric: 138 over the square root of epsilon r, times the logarithm of D over d. The velocity factor is the phase velocity over the speed of light, one over the square root of epsilon r: 66 % for solid polyethylene and 69 % for PTFE. The capacitance per metre is 24.12 epsilon r over the logarithm of D over d picofarads per metre, which can also be written 3326 times the square root of epsilon r over Z zero. Combining them, the characteristic impedance is one over the phase velocity times the capacitance, so a datasheet that gives the velocity factor and the capacitance gives you Z zero. For the LMR-400 cable in the brochure, 84 % and 78.4 picofarads per metre give 50.6 ohms: it is a 50 ohm cable. The same glossary also gives the attenuation in dB per 100 m as a conductor term, 1.43 R over Z zero, plus a dielectric term, 9.15 root epsilon r times the frequency in megahertz times the loss factor F, so loss grows with frequency, which is why datasheets quote dB per 100 feet at each frequency.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 2: Línies de transmissió i sistemes guiats (2026), slides 16–17 (technical glossary); Times Microwave LMR-FR brochure

16. Other kinds of transmission line

4 · Coaxial cables
  • Waveguide: 1–300 GHz, high power, very low loss, bulky
  • Coaxial cable: up to ≈ 50 GHz, Z₀ 10–600 Ω (in practice 50 / 75 Ω), shielded
  • Stripline, microstrip, coplanar waveguide, slotline: planar lines on PCBs, cheap, compatible with active devices
  • Microstrip: εeff between 1 and εr (part of the field is in air)
Narration

The slides close the line section with a comparison table. Waveguides work from 1 to 300 GHz and handle high power with very low loss, but they are bulky and expensive. Coaxial cables work up to about 50 GHz, are shielded, and are made almost always at 50 or 75 ohms. Planar lines, stripline, microstrip, coplanar waveguide and slotline, are printed on circuit boards: small, cheap and easy to connect to transistors, which is why every RF circuit board uses them. In a microstrip part of the field travels in the air above the board, so its effective permittivity lies between one and that of the substrate, which is why the matching problems give you an epsilon effective.

17. The Smith chart

5 · Smith chart
  • Polar plot of Γ; grid of normalised z = Z/Z₀ = (1 + Γ)/(1 − Γ)
  • Circles of constant r and arcs of constant x; upper half inductive, lower half capacitive
  • Centre: matched (Γ = 0); left: short (Γ = −1); right: open (Γ = +1)
  • Moving along the line: rotate on the |Γ| circle, CLOCKWISE towards the generator; one turn = λg/2
  • SWR: where the |Γ| circle cuts the right half of the real axis (read r)
  • Admittance y: rotate 180° (λg/4), the point opposite z

Equations

z=Z0​Z​=1−Γ1+Γ​,Γ=z+1z−1​,y=z1​  (the point opposite z)
Narration

The Smith chart is a graphical calculator for transmission lines. It is a polar plot of the reflection coefficient, on which a grid shows the normalised impedance, z equal to Z over Z zero, which is one plus gamma over one minus gamma. The circles are lines of constant normalised resistance and the arcs are lines of constant reactance: the upper half is inductive and the lower half capacitive. The centre is the matched point, the left end of the axis is the short circuit and the right end the open circuit. Since moving along the line only changes the phase of gamma, moving from the load towards the generator is a clockwise rotation on a circle centred at the origin, and a full turn is half a wavelength; the outer scale is graduated in wavelengths for this reason. Where that circle crosses the right half of the real axis, the value of r read there is the SWR. And because a quarter wavelength inverts the impedance, the normalised admittance is the point diametrically opposite the impedance, a 180 degree rotation.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 2: Línies de transmissió i sistemes guiats (2026), slides 20–22

20. Scattering parameters

6 · S-parameters
  • Z and Y matrices need opens/shorts: hard at RF; S is measured with a vector network analyser
  • a_i = V_i⁺/√Z₀ᵢ incident, b_i = V_i⁻/√Z₀ᵢ reflected; [b] = [S][a]
  • S_ij = b_i / a_j with all other ports MATCHED (a_k = 0)
  • S₁₁, S₂₂: reflection coefficients; S₂₁: forward transmission (gain), S₁₂: reverse
  • In dB: |S₁₁| → return loss = −20 log|S₁₁|; |S₂₁|² → gain or insertion loss

Equations

[b1​b2​​]=[S11​S21​​S12​S22​​][a1​a2​​],Sij​=aj​bi​​​ak​=0, k=j​
Narration

At high frequency impedance and admittance matrices are difficult to measure directly, because they need ideal open or short circuits at the ports, which are hard to realise, so we describe networks with scattering parameters, which a vector network analyser measures directly. At each port we define a normalised incident wave a and a reflected wave b, the voltage waves divided by the square root of the port impedance, and the scattering matrix relates them: b equals S times a. Each parameter S i j is b i over a j, measured with all the other ports terminated in matched loads, so that nothing comes back into them. For a two-port, S one one and S two two are the reflection coefficients at the input and the output, S two one is the forward transmission, the gain of an amplifier, and S one two the reverse transmission. That is why last year's exam gave the amplifier gains as S two one equal to 10 dB, and why minus 20 log of S one one is the return loss.

Sources: Gil, SRFC Mòdul 2: Línies de transmissió i sistemes guiats (2026), slides 26–27

21. Why and how to match

7 · Matching
  • Maximum power to the load; better SNR in receivers (antenna–LNA); less wasted power in transmitters
  • Lumped L-section (two reactances): practical for f ≲ 1 GHz
  • z_L inside the r = 1 circle (R_L > Z₀): shunt element at the LOAD, then series
  • z_L outside it (R_L < Z₀): series element at the LOAD, then shunt
  • Two solutions each; then X = x Z₀ → L = X/ω or C = 1/(ω|X|); B = b/Z₀ → C = B/ω or L = 1/(ω|B|)
  • Quarter-wave transformer: real loads, narrow band

Equations

B=RL2​+XL2​XL​±RL​/Z0​​RL2​+XL2​−Z0​RL​​​,X=B1​+RL​XL​Z0​​−BRL​Z0​​
R_L > Z₀: shunt B at the load, then series X

Narration

Matching networks transform the load so that the line sees Z zero. That delivers maximum power to the load, improves the signal-to-noise ratio of a receiver when the antenna is matched to the LNA, and in a transmitter minimises wasted power, saving battery and reducing radiation hazards. The simplest network is the L-section, two reactive elements, which is practical with lumped components up to about 1 GHz. If the normalised load lies inside the r equals one circle, that is if R L is larger than Z zero, the element next to the load is in shunt and the second one in series. If it lies outside, R L smaller than Z zero, the element next to the load is in series and the second one in shunt. Each case has two solutions; de-normalise the reactance by multiplying by Z zero and the susceptance by dividing by Z zero, and turn a positive reactance into an inductor and a negative one into a capacitor, while a positive susceptance is a capacitor and a negative one an inductor. For real loads there is also the quarter-wave transformer, which we will see after the L-section examples.

26. Traps that cost points

Summary
  • βl uses the GUIDED wavelength; lengths repeat every λg/2 (0.7λ ≡ 0.2λ)
  • Γ rotates by 2βl (720° per λ), CLOCKWISE towards the generator
  • |Γ| from SWR: (SWR − 1)/(SWR + 1); P_r = |Γ|²; RL = −20 log|Γ|
  • Normalise before the chart; de-normalise after (Z × Z₀, Y ÷ Z₀)
  • L-section: inside r = 1 → shunt at the load; outside → series at the load
  • Coax: Z₀ = 1/(v_p C) is quickest; v_p = VF · c
Narration

Before the summary, the traps in this unit. Beta l uses the guided wavelength, not the free-space one, and everything repeats every half wavelength. Gamma rotates by twice beta l, that is 720 degrees per wavelength, clockwise when you move towards the generator. From the SWR, gamma is SWR minus one over SWR plus one; the reflected power fraction is gamma squared, and the return loss is minus 20 log of gamma, not 10 log. On the Smith chart, normalise before you start and de-normalise at the end, multiplying impedances by Z zero and dividing admittances by it. For an L-section, check where the load is: inside the r equals one circle, the shunt element goes at the load; outside, the series element does. And for a coaxial cable from a datasheet, Z zero equals one over the phase velocity times the capacitance is the quickest route.
